When these letters are in lower case, migrants of that category may occur alongside resident,
non-migratory individuals or individuals of one of the other migrant categories. When they are
in round brackets, some birds in Kenya might be migrants or wanderers from that particular
region or area.
   If possible, where there are fewer than four records, these are listed under the species name.
Where no reference is given, the record is usually listed in Zimmerman et al. (1996) or has been
accepted by the East Africa Rarities Committee (EARC).
   The BirdLife/IUCN criteria for assigning threat status and category (Critically Endangered,
Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, and Data Deficient) are included where appropriate.
1
 ‘Migrant’, in the accepted ornithological sense, means a bird that moves on a regular, seasonal basis from
one, to one or more defined areas; thus, the majority of wader species that breed in the High Arctic leave
their breeding areas to spend most of the year on coasts and wetlands throughout the temperate and tropical
areas of the world; they are therefore true migrants. On the other hand, Little Grebes that travel, often at
night, in search of newly formed ponds, are wanderers, not migrants.

Struthionidae: Ostrich
c Ostrich Struthio camelus
   In the 1996 list, Struthio (camelus) molybdophanes, English name the Somali Ostrich, was treated as a separate
   species (Freitag & Robinson 1993), necessitating the qualifier ‘Common’ for the species containing the other
   two races (nominate camelus and massaicus); with the lumping in the present list no qualifier is required

About this list
With 1100 species, a rich variety of habitats, and relative ease of travel and access to
its many National Parks and reserves, Kenya is one of the main countries in Africa
for bird watching. For its many visitors and for increasing numbers of local enthusi-
asts, an updated version of the country’s bird list has become essential.
    More than a decade has passed since the Bird Committee (or Ornithological
Sub-committee as it was then called) last revised this list. In the meantime, avian
systematics and taxonomy have moved on rapidly, and species and genus changes
are constantly being suggested from increasing numbers of molecular studies. Some
surprising results are emerging from the DNA sequencing of more and more groups
of birds. There will never be a perfect time for a review of the list, and the present
offering is an attempt to keep up with the more generally accepted changes, bearing
in mind that further revision will be needed in a few years’ time.
    The starting point for species and genera was our own 1996 list. This was reviewed
in the light of decisions made in the later volumes of Birds of Africa (e.g., Fry &
Keith (Eds) 2004) and those of the third edition of ‘Howard & Moore’ (Dickinson
2003). We have adopted a number of more recently suggested amendments, but
have tended to take a cautious approach to new species-splits and generic arrange-
ments. We have avoided changes where questions clearly remain or where we feel
that wider acceptance is still needed. Our judgement of species limits has continued
to be based on the biological species concept. The current total of 1100 accepted
species may well be fewer than in some lists used by today’s birders.
    The order of families differs from the traditional one used for the previous list.
It follows the ‘gamebirds-and-ducks-first’ sequence of Howard & Moore, which
was informed by up-to-date genetic studies, and is now being used increasingly.
But even this order is bound to be modified as a result of the recent DNA work by
Hackett et al. (2008).
    Our approach to English names has been conservative. We have been reluctant
to move away from well-established names, and have done so only in favour of an
alternative which we regard as being clearly better or more appropriate, or where
a species-split has made a new name necessary. In an attempt to standardize, the
International Ornithological Congress has published a recommended list of world
English bird names (Gill & Wright 2006). We have changed some of our names
to conform with suggested ones in this list, but in other cases we have preferred
to retain our own name; in some, but not all, cases, this has been noted. We have
generally used as few hyphens in group names as possible.

The following species are not included in the checklist for the reasons given.
Mascarene Shearwater Puffinus atrodorsalis
   It was included in the 1996 list in square brackets, i.e., there was insufficient evidence. It is now considered a
   race of Audubon’s Shearwater (Bretagnolle et al. 2000).
Red-tailed Tropicbird Phaethon rubricauda
   It was included in the 1996 list in square brackets. Although there have been unsubstantiated sightings from
   Kenyan waters, there is no confirmed record to date.
Eurasian Griffon Gyps fulvus
   Although details of a January 2000 sighting from Amboseli National Park have appeared in the literature (Bul-
   letin of the African Bird Club 8: 59), the details have not yet been submitted to the EARC. It was called the
   Griffon Vulture by Gill & Wright (2006).
Little Gull Hydrocoloeus minutus
   The record of c. 60 at Lake Turkana in January 1979 (Groh 1979) has been rejected (see Zimmerman et al.
   (1996) — where it is called Larus minutus — for more detail).
Mediterranean Gull Larus melanocephalus
   Lake Turkana and Lamu sightings are considered dubious, see Zimmerman et al. (1996).
White-eyed Gull Larus leucophthalmus
   It was included in the 1996 list in square brackets; the claimed record from Lake Turkana remains unsubstanti-
   ated; see Zimmerman et al. (1996) for more detail.
Arctic Tern Sterna paradisaea
   Although details and photographs of one from the Sabaki River Mouth (6 July 2002) have appeared in the
   literature (Bisschop 2002 and [Bisschop 2003]) this observation has not been submitted to the EARC.
Catharacta skuas
   There have been several coastal sightings of large brown skuas belonging to this genus, but none has been
   substantiated (Zimmerman et al. 1996). Gill & Wright (2006) do not admit this genus.
Pallid Swift Apus pallidus
   The EARC’s decision on a claimed record has been put on hold pending “expert opinion”.
Boubous Laniarius spp.
   The Tropical Boubou Laniarius aethiopicus has recently been split by Nguembock et al. (2008) into several
   taxa, including what they treat as three full species which occur in Kenya. It is possible that these three species
   will find acceptance by the authors of the next edition of Dickinson (2003) due to be published in 2010. The
   most widespread of these new taxa is the Tropical Boubou Laniarius major; in the far north of the country,
   around Moyale, the Ethiopian Boubou L. aethiopicaus occurs, while the coastal lowlands are occupied by the
   Dimorphic Boubou L. sublacteus. In the present list we remain conservative, retaining Laniarius aethiopicus,
   the Tropical Boubou.
Melodious Warbler Hippolais polyglotta
   A bird identified as this species was caught, photographed and ringed at Ngulia, Tsavo on 19 November 1995
   (Pearson et al. 1998). It was determined on plumage and biometric characters and, in addition, an analysis of
   mitrochondrial DNA carried out in the laboratory of Dr Andreas Helbig confirmed its maternal identity. After
   the death of Dr Helbig, information has come to hand that the information sent to David Pearson was incor-
   rect (Gerhard Nikolaus in litt.). The species was not included in the 1996 list.
Forest Robin Stiphrornis erythrothorax
   It was included in the 1996 list in square brackets but the specimen labelled “Kipkabus, nr Eldoret 16 April
   1966” collected by Brian Heath, is considered to have been labelled erroneously. The specimen is suspected to
   have originated in western Uganda.

Rüppell’s Weaver Ploceus galbula
    Inclusion in the 1996 list was based on a 1901 female specimen from the Kenya–Ethiopian border area (Ash
    1988). However, John Miskell (pers. comm.) considers the record dubious as he believes Erlanger (1907) to

    Southern Somalia and the Kenya–Ethiopian border may have been mis-identified.
‘Nairobi Pipit’
    From 1994, Brian Finch has noted pipits in Nairobi National Park which he, and others, believe to be an
    undescribed species, the ‘Nairobi Pipit’. The bird is frequently mentioned in websites and an article on it has
    been published (Finch 2002). However, this bird has not been scientifically described or named, nor has it
    been submitted to the EARC.

Nature Kenya — the East Africa Natural History Society
Nature Kenya is the East Africa Natural History Society (EANHS) in Kenya. It is a member-
ship-based organization, founded in 1909. Nature Kenya invites all who are not members to
join and be part of a strong and active society where members are encouraged to participate
in the study of nature and help in the implementation of conservation activities.
   Connecting nature and people to take action for biodiversity conservation is the Society’s mis-
sion statement. Nature Kenya strives to:
    • Build a strong network of people for conservation across the country
    • Enhance knowledge of Kenya’s biodiversity
    • Advocate policies favourable to biodiversity conservation
    • Promote the conservation of key species, sites, and habitats
    • Encourage community participation in conservation through the promotion of
       sustainable benefits.
