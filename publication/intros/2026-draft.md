# Welcome

Welcome to the *Checklist of the Birds of Kenya — 2026 Draft*, prepared towards the Sixth Edition.

This draft checklist follows AviList v2026, a new world list of birds that readers can view and download at [www.avilist.org](https://www.avilist.org/), for taxonomic identity, sequence, family placement, and default English and scientific names. AviList aims to unify the world’s three most widely used bird lists — BirdLife/Handbook of the Birds of the World, IOC, and Clements/Cornell — into a single agreed list of the world’s bird species. This will help to align global data and conservation resources, such as the IUCN Red List, eBird, the BirdLife DataZone, and Cornell’s Birds of the World, around a single checklist. It will be updated each year.

You will notice a different sequence of families and a few birds with new names. Name changes happen when scientists find that a bird belongs to a different species, genus, or family. Sometimes subspecies or populations are split into new species. These changes are generally based on new information, including DNA evidence. For instance, falcons are now understood to be more closely related to parrots than to other birds of prey.

There is also an effort to choose one international English name for birds that may have different common names in different countries. This is still ongoing, and African birders and ornithologists are communicating with AviList.

If there is a bird name you cannot find, look at the bird’s family and compare the scientific name with the one in your bird guidebook or the fifth Kenya Checklist. That may not always work, as scientific names also change with new research. In that case, look up the bird online on AviList, BirdLife DataZone, or eBird. A comparison of the changes between the fifth Kenya Checklist (2019) and this draft is available at [a-rocha-kenya.github.io/Birds-of-Kenya](https://a-rocha-kenya.github.io/Birds-of-Kenya/downloads/Taxonomy-changes-2019.1-to-2026.0.pdf).

In the case of lovebirds, Fischer’s Lovebird and Yellow-collared Lovebird are listed as naturalised, and there is no space for “Hybrid Lovebird”. The taxonomy of these lovebirds is still under discussion. All we can say for now is that they came from Tanzania, are now widespread in Kenya, and many may be hybrids descended from escaped cage birds.

We hope to update this draft to a more complete and detailed checklist soon.
